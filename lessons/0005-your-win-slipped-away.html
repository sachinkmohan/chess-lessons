Your Win Slipped Away

You had Queen + Rook + Bishop against a lone king. The game ended in a draw. One two-second check would have prevented it.

The story in brief

You played the Scandinavian as Black. By move 49, queens were traded and you reached a pure endgame with this material count:

♛ Queen   ♜ Rook   ♝ Bishop   + 3 pawns
vs
♔ King   ♗ Bishop   ♟ one pawn

You were completely winning. Then, with seven moves left to go, you walked into a trap you'd already seen in Lesson 2.

The fatal position — move 56

After a long shuffle, White's king retreated to h3. Their last active piece was a bishop, sitting on f4. Your rook was right on f5, one square away. Free bishop — take it, right?

You played 56…Rxf4 — and the game was immediately declared a draw by stalemate.

Count White’s legal moves after 56…Rxf4:
  • Kh3 → h4 — your rook on f4 controls the entire 4th rank. ✗ Blocked.
  • Kh3 → g4 — rook on f4 controls g4. ✗ Blocked.
  • Kh3 → g3 — your queen on g1 controls the g-file. ✗ Blocked.
  • Kh3 → g2 — queen controls g2. ✗ Blocked.
  • Kh3 → h2 — queen on g1 attacks h2 diagonally. ✗ Blocked.
  • Pawn h6 → h7 — your king sits on h7. ✗ Blocked.
Zero legal moves. King not in check. Stalemate — automatic draw.

Puzzle 1 — find the winning move

You’re back in the same position, before the blunder. Your queen is on g1, rook on f5. What do you play?

Why Qg2+ wins cleanly

After 56…Qg2+, the White king is in check — it must move. The only safe square is h4. Now the stalemate danger is gone: the king has room. You play 57…Rxf4+, winning the bishop with check. The king keeps moving, and you convert your material advantage normally.

The Stalemate Check
Before capturing your opponent’s last active piece, pause and ask:

“After I take this, can the enemy king move anywhere?”

If you’re not sure — deliver a check first. A check forces the king to a new square, opening up escape routes. Then capture. Never the other way around.

Puzzle 2 — apply the rule in a new position

Classic K+Q vs K. Your queen is on a5, your king on b3. The White king is cornered on a1. One natural-looking queen move ends in an immediate draw. Find the winning move.

The other side of stalemate

This lesson is about avoiding stalemate when you’re winning. But the same rule is a weapon when you’re losing: if your king has no moves and you have no pieces, try to manoeuvre into a position where your opponent might accidentally stalemate you. It can turn a certain loss into a draw. You don’t need to know this deeply yet — just note that it exists.

Your pre-capture habit. Whenever you’re about to take the opponent’s last piece, freeze and count their king’s legal squares. If the count is zero or might become zero after your move — give a check first. This takes two seconds and saves games.

What else came up in these games

Twelve rapid games, Jun 26–30. The stalemate was the clearest lesson. Two other patterns worth noting, for future sessions:

  • Time trouble (game vs vihu49). You had 2 seconds left in a complex position and lost on the clock — despite having reasonable material. The habit to build: after move 20, glance at the clock every few moves.
  • Greedy captures early (game vs martinon2). You took the c2 pawn on move 7 with your bishop — opening up lines against your own king. The same pattern from Lesson 4, this time with a bishop. If a pawn grab moves a piece toward the centre of the opponent’s active pieces, it’s probably a trap.
