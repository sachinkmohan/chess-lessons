The f6 Fire Alarm

You were checkmated in 23 moves, your king boxed in its own corner. The alarm went off long before the mate — a single enemy pawn told you the attack was coming.

How it ended

You castled your king to safety on the kingside. Twenty-three moves in, that “safe” king was checkmated by a single rook:

The real culprit: the pawn on f6

A pawn that reaches f6 (or f3 against you) right next to a castled king is one of the most dangerous things in chess. From f6 it attacks two squares: g7 and e7. Those are exactly the squares your king needs to breathe — and the squares it needs to recapture an invading rook. With f6 in place, your g7 pawn becomes glued in front of your king, unable to move, just waiting to be smashed.

The break-in — an open g-file did the rest

Earlier in the game you traded a bishop with 13…Bxf3, and White recaptured 14.gxf3. That opened the g-file — a clear lane pointing straight at your king. White stacked a rook on it (20.Rhg1) and you walked into the storm:

Puzzle 1 — see the attack with the attacker’s eyes

Learn the pattern by playing it. You’re White here. Black’s king looks tucked away behind its g7 pawn. The g-file is open and your pawn sits on f6. Find the breakthrough.

Why the sacrifice works

This is the exact sequence that ended your game. Walk through it slowly — notice that the king can never take the rook, because the f6 pawn guards g7:

  • 21.Rxg7+ The rook gives itself up. Your king can’t recapture — f6 covers g7. The king must run.
  • 21…Kf8 The only flight square.
  • 22.Rh7 The rook slides over, threatening Rh8#.
  • 22…Re6 Too slow — nothing defends the back rank.
  • 23.Rh8# Checkmate. f6 covers e7 and g7; the rook covers the rest.
The f6 Fire Alarm
When an enemy pawn reaches f6 next to your castled king (or f3 when you’re White), treat it as an alarm bell. It freezes your g7 pawn and steals your king’s escape squares. Two responses:

• Don’t let it land for free — challenge or capture the pawn before it digs in.
• Watch the open file next to your king. A rook arriving on it means a sacrifice on g7 is in the air.

Puzzle 2 — prevent it (rewind to move 13)

Here’s where the trouble really started. Your bishop on g4 attacks the knight on f3. Taking it feels natural — but think about how White must recapture, and which file that opens, toward whose king. Choose the move that keeps your king’s shelter intact.

The principle: don’t help your opponent open lines toward your own king. A trade that forces gxf3 hands White a free highway down the g-file — the very lane the mating rook travelled. When you’re castled, keep the files in front of your king closed.
